01
Why quantum?

Two reasons ordinary computers are running out of room

Why quantum computing?

Limit 1: chips

Transistors are now only a few dozen atoms wide. Any smaller and quantum effects break the circuit.

Limit 2: nature

To imitate a quantum system, every extra particle doubles the numbers a normal computer must store.

Rice on a chessboard

Each square doubles the one before. A quantum system does the same with every extra particle.

“…if you want to make a simulation of nature, you'd better make it quantum mechanical.”

—Richard Feynman (lecture 1981, published 1982)

The takeaway

A specialist for certain problems, not a faster laptop.

02
One qubit

What a qubit is, and what happens when you read it

Bit vs qubit

A bit is a switch. A qubit is an arrow that can point anywhere on a sphere.

Each part of the mix has a weight. The bigger the weight, the more likely you read that value.

Physicists write the two basic states as |0⟩ and |1⟩. The brackets are just name tags.

0 and 1 at the same time

Superposition: a qubit holding 0 and 1 at once, each with its own weight.

Weights can be positive or negative. Ordinary chances can't be. This is what makes quantum different.

Plus and minus both read as 50% 0 and 50% 1, yet they are different states.

Two common myths

Picturing a qubit

The Bloch sphere. Quantum gates simply rotate this arrow (Part 2). It works for one qubit only.

  • North pole = 0, south pole = 1, equator = the 50/50 mixes
  • Height sets the chance of reading 0 or 1
  • Direction around is the phase: a plain reading can't see it, but it changes what happens next
  • Plus and minus: same height, opposite direction
The math of one qubit

A qubit: |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩ with |α|² + |β|² = 1. Reading gives 0 with chance |α|² and 1 with chance |β|².

plus = (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2, minus = (|0⟩ − |1⟩)/√2. On the sphere: |ψ⟩ = cos(θ/2)|0⟩ + eiφ sin(θ/2)|1⟩, with θ the height and φ the phase.

Source: Nielsen & Chuang (2010), Fig. 1.3.

The hidden direction

Reading: a one-way door

After reading, the arrow snaps to a pole and everything else is lost.

Random: even if you know the qubit exactly, you only know the odds, never the single result.

Collapse: after reading, the qubit becomes the answer. The mix is gone for good.

A huge store, a tiny door

Weights act like waves

Weights can cancel

Equal signs add up, opposite signs cancel.

Add one extra step (a gate called H) before reading. Each result can then be reached along two paths.

100%

The key idea

Ordinary chances only add up. Quantum weights can cancel.

Algorithms boost the right answer and cancel the wrong ones.

Many qubits

How qubits combine, and what they can never do

From one qubit to many

This is the doubling from the start. Entanglement is a big reason quantum computers are so hard to imitate.

Independent qubits: each can be described on its own. Easy for a normal computer.

Entangled qubits: only the whole group can be described. All 2n weights are needed.

Two gloves in two boxes?

Bell's game

Bell's game, also called the CHSH game.

Alice and Bob each get question A or B at random and answer 0 or 1, with no talking.

They win if the answers match, unless both got B: then the answers must differ.

The most quantum physics allows

Bell test result

Entanglement is not answers fixed in advance. It is a genuinely new resource.

Confirmed by real experiments: Nobel Prize 2022 (Aspect, Clauser, Zeilinger).

Math: many qubits, Bell

The numbers behind the last few slides:

  • n qubits: 2n complex weights; independent qubits need only 2 per qubit
  • Bell pair: (|00⟩ + |11⟩)/√2
  • Bell's game: best fixed plan 75%; entangled pair cos²(π/8) ≈ 85.4%, the most quantum physics allows

You cannot copy a qubit

Reading first doesn't help: one reading gives one bit and destroys the mix.

Rule: no machine can copy an unknown qubit.

A machine that copies 0 and 1 correctly turns a mix into a linked pair, not two copies.

Bits are fine, which is why ordinary data can be copied freely.

Math: why copying fails

A copier that maps |0⟩|0⟩ → |0⟩|0⟩ and |1⟩|0⟩ → |1⟩|1⟩ must, by linearity, send α|0⟩ + β|1⟩ to α|00⟩ + β|11⟩: an entangled pair (circuit on the next slide).

A true copy would be (α|0⟩ + β|1⟩)(α|0⟩ + β|1⟩), a different state. So no such copier can exist.

Source: Nielsen & Chuang (2010), Box 12.1; Wootters & Zurek (1982).
